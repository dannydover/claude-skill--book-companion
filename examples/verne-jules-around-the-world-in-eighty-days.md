# *Around the World in Eighty Days* by Jules Verne — originally in French

## At a Glance

- **Length:** roughly 64,000 words; 37 chapters; about 240 pages in the recommended edition.
- **Reading time:** four to five hours at a comfortable pace.
- **Structure:** 37 short chapters, six to eight pages each, written for daily newspaper serialization. Most chapters close on a small hook engineered to carry the reader from one morning's installment to the next.
- **Difficulty:** low. Plain nineteenth-century narrative prose, occasional untranslated French phrases (handled in any annotated edition), and almost no interior monologue to follow. Closer to Conan Doyle than to Hugo or Flaubert.
- **Best read:** in short sittings, one or two chapters at a time. Reading it in a single sustained burst flattens the daily-cliffhanger rhythm Verne built into it; the pacing is half the pleasure.

## Why This Book Is Worth Reading (or might not be)

The book is worth reading because it documents — in the form of a wager — the precise moment the planet became measurable. Three pieces of infrastructure had clicked into place inside thirty-six months: the Suez Canal opened in November 1869, the American transcontinental railroad was completed in May 1869, and the Indian railway network linked Bombay to Calcutta via Allahabad on 7 March 1870. Verne sat down to write within two years of the third event. The novel is the moment those three engineering facts entered the imagination as a single connected system, and you can feel the giddiness of that recognition on every page. It is, among other things, the first global novel — in the literal sense that its setting is the globe, treated as a single navigable object.

What carries it past its historical moment is Phileas Fogg himself: a man whose only stated emotion is a wish to be on time. He is one of the great inscrutable figures in popular fiction — a human metronome moved through landscapes designed to resist him — and the book's deepest pleasure is the friction between his clockwork interior and an exterior world that keeps insisting on being unpredictable. The plotting is propulsive, the geography is exact, and the prose, while functional, never gets in its own way.

It might not be for you if you want interiority, ambiguity, or psychological depth. Fogg is a mechanism by design; his servant Passepartout is the only character with anything like an inner life, and even that is sketched rather than developed. Long passages of the journey through India, southeast Asia, Japan, and the American West are filtered through a colonial gaze Verne does not examine and that a modern reader has to actively work around. The prose, even in a good translation, is serviceable rather than beautiful — Verne was a plotter, not a stylist. If your benchmark for nineteenth-century adventure is *Treasure Island* or *The Count of Monte Cristo*, this compares well. If it is *Middlemarch* or *Anna Karenina*, you have the wrong book in your hand.

### Cultural Impact

The novel's most striking afterlife is that people did the journey to test it. In November 1889, the American journalist Nellie Bly left Hoboken aboard the steamer *Augusta Victoria* under commission from Joseph Pulitzer's *New York World*, with the explicit goal of beating Fogg's fictional record. She returned to New Jersey on 25 January 1890 having circled the globe in 72 days, 6 hours, and 11 minutes. The *World* ran a "Bly Guessing Match," inviting readers to predict her finish time to the second; the prize evolved into a paid European trip. The rival magazine *Cosmopolitan* dispatched its own reporter, Elizabeth Bisland, eastbound to Bly's westbound, in a parallel circumnavigation that became one of the great press spectacles of the 1880s. Bisland finished four days behind. Few works of fiction have generated this kind of literal field-test — readers running the wager into the actual world to see whether the book was right.

The 1956 Hollywood adaptation, produced by Mike Todd and directed by Michael Anderson, won five Academy Awards including Best Picture in a year when it beat *Giant*, *The King and I*, and *The Ten Commandments*. It was shot across 112 locations in thirteen countries, employed nearly 69,000 extras, and pioneered the practice of stunt-casting "cameo" appearances — Frank Sinatra, Marlene Dietrich, Buster Keaton, John Gielgud, Noël Coward, and dozens more. David Niven's Fogg and Cantinflas's Passepartout supplied the cultural image of the book that most people who have not read it still carry around in their heads: the journey as romantic spectacle, the Englishman as imperturbable gentleman.

The title itself has become a productive phrase. "Around the world in X days" is now a generic template for any record-setting circumnavigation — by balloon, bicycle, solo sailboat, commercial aircraft — and the phrase works independently of any knowledge of Verne. The book also established the globe-trotting race as a recognizable narrative form, with a fixed clock, a fixed endpoint, and a sequence of exotic obstacles; that structure is the direct ancestor of countless adventure novels, action films, and televised reality formats.

## Then and Now: How This Book Was Read in 1872

Modern readers come to *Around the World in Eighty Days* as either a children's adventure or an antique romp. Almost the opposite is closer to how its first readers experienced it.

To a Frenchman opening *Le Temps* in November 1872, the novel was something like contemporary realism. The story began serialization on 6 November 1872 and ran for 45 days, the final installment appearing on 22 December — one day after Fogg's fictional return deadline of 21 December. Verne and his publisher had engineered a piece of fiction whose plot ticked in real time alongside the reader's own calendar. Each morning's installment landed on the breakfast table on roughly the day Fogg was supposed to be at that point in the journey. Contemporary accounts suggest that some readers came to believe a real journey was underway; wagers were placed, and steamship and railway companies are reported to have lobbied Verne for placement in the narrative. The closest modern analogue is a podcast or streaming series releasing episodes in sync with real events its audience is tracking — the "is this happening?" effect was central to the original experience and is almost completely lost in book form today.

The political backdrop has also faded. France in 1872 was a humiliated nation. The Franco-Prussian War had ended in May 1871 with the Treaty of Frankfurt, which imposed an indemnity of five billion francs and amputated Alsace-Lorraine — 1,694 villages and cities — from French territory. German troops remained on French soil pending payment and would not fully withdraw until September 1873. The Paris Commune had been crushed in late May 1871 in a week of street fighting (la semaine sanglante) that left much of central Paris in ruins. National confidence was in pieces. Into that mood Verne dropped a novel in which a French valet, Passepartout, is the warm and resourceful human heart of a journey otherwise propelled by an English bachelor's money and English mechanical efficiency. Verne, who loved English literature and had spent time in Britain, was not anti-English; but his gentle teasing of Reform Club complacency and Fogg's clockwork affect would have read very differently to a Parisian audience smarting from defeat than it reads to us.

The technological context is equally dimmed. Verne's premise was not a fantasy in 1872; it was a current-events claim. The Suez Canal had been one of the great spectacles of the age when it opened in November 1869. The completion of the American transcontinental railroad at Promontory Summit the same year was front-page news worldwide. The Bombay-to-Calcutta railway link via Allahabad had clicked into place in March 1870. And in September 1872 — six weeks before serialization began — Thomas Cook himself, aged 63, had departed Leicester on the first commercially organized round-the-world tour, a 222-day excursion advertised in the British press through the spring and summer. Verne, in interviews in 1894 and 1904, attributed the spark for the novel variously to "reading one day in a Paris café" a piece in *Le Siècle* about a man who could now travel around the globe in eighty days, and to "a tourist advertisement seen by chance in the columns of a newspaper" — likely Cook's. The novel was, in effect, asking its readers to evaluate a claim being made by actual travel companies in actual newspapers: can you really do this now? The answer mattered to the original audience in a way it cannot quite matter to us.

The colonial frame, which today reads as the book's most uncomfortable feature, was for the original audience simply the texture of the world. The European reader of 1872 took for granted that the planet was knit together by a network of British, French, and American steamers and railways operated by Europeans for European convenience, and that the local populations were, narratively, scenery and obstacle. Verne is no more colonial than his readership; what dates is not the prejudice but the unselfconsciousness of it. A modern reader does interpretive work the original reader did not have to do — seeing the colonial machinery as machinery rather than as the natural order of things.

Finally, what is invisible to a modern reader is how Verne was originally classified. He was read in 1872 as a modernist, in the literal sense — a writer of the modern, the contemporary, the just-now-possible. He was neither nostalgic nor futuristic. He was reporting on the present and finding it astonishing. The label "father of science fiction" is posthumous: Hugo Gernsback, the American pulp publisher, founded *Amazing Stories* in April 1926 and used a stylized image of Verne's tomb as its emblem, retroactively annexing him to a genre that had not existed during his lifetime. *Eighty Days* contains no science fiction at all. Every train, ship, and elephant in the novel was available for hire in 1872. The wonder Verne wants you to feel is not the wonder of imagined futures but the wonder of the actual present — the realization that this is where we now live, a planet whose far side is eighty days away. To recover the book's first force, try reading it not as old adventure but as a piece of journalism about a world that had just, for the first time in human history, become finite.

## Writing the Book

Verne wrote *Around the World in Eighty Days* in 1872 in Amiens, the provincial cathedral city in Picardy where his wife Honorine's family was rooted. He had moved his household there from the Paris region in 1871, in the aftermath of the worst eighteen months in nineteenth-century French history: the disastrous war with Prussia of 1870–71, the loss of Alsace-Lorraine, the five-billion-franc indemnity, and the Commune's collapse in a week of street fighting that had left central Paris smouldering. Verne was forty-four, settled with his wife and their son Michel, recently honored as a Knight of the Legion of Honour (1870), and writing inside the long commercial contract with Pierre-Jules Hetzel that had structured every book of the *Voyages Extraordinaires* since *Five Weeks in a Balloon* in 1863. The contract was generous but demanding; Hetzel set the pace and shaped the manuscripts.

The novel was written for serial publication in the Paris daily *Le Temps*, with installments running from 6 November to 22 December 1872. The deadline pressure was structural: the plot's ticking clock was also the author's, and Verne was composing ahead of publication in real time. The book shows it. There is almost no slack in any chapter; each closes on a turn calibrated to pull the reader into the next morning's installment. Hetzel, an experienced serial publisher, pushed Verne hard on momentum, and the manuscript was simultaneously being typeset, illustrated planning was underway, and the book was being plotted toward the calendrical coincidence with the reader's December. Verne reportedly enjoyed insisting to friends, mid-composition, that Fogg would make it back in time. Bets were placed.

The connection to the conditions of writing is audible in retrospect. A country that had just experienced the total failure of its military and administrative machinery — trains seized, timetables in pieces, the infrastructure of the modern state visibly shaken — was handed a novel in which the machinery works, the timetables hold, and a man who trusts the system completely is vindicated by it. Whether Verne intended that consolation or simply wrote the book that interested him, the timing gave the novel an undertone of recovered confidence that the first readers would have felt without necessarily naming. The novel's unembarrassed optimism about steam, schedules, and the modern world is, on the calendar, a fact about a country that had recently watched its capital burn.

### Recommended English Translations

The translation situation in Verne is unusually consequential. Most editions of his work sold in English from the 1870s through the late twentieth century were heavily abridged and frequently inaccurate; nineteenth-century English-language publishers cut, paraphrased, and occasionally rewrote whole passages in pursuit of a children's market. A scholarly recovery has been underway since the 1990s. Buy a modern critical translation if you can.

- *Around the World in Eighty Days*, trans. William Butcher (Oxford World's Classics, 1995; reissued 1999, with a revised text in the 2008 *Extraordinary Journeys* relaunch). The first true critical edition in English — Butcher is one of the leading Anglophone Verne scholars — with an extensive introduction, a chronology, manuscript variants, and detailed notes on nineteenth-century references. If you read only one edition, read this one.
- *Around the World in Eighty Days*, trans. Michael Glencross (Penguin Classics, 2004), with an introduction by Brian Aldiss. A good modern alternative; Glencross is a specialist in nineteenth-century French literature, and the Penguin edition is more widely stocked than the Oxford.
- *Around the World in Eighty Days*, trans. George Makepeace Towle (James R. Osgood, Boston, 1873). The original English translation, and the one most cheap public-domain reprints still use. It is competent for its period but loose, occasionally clipped, and now reads as dated. Treat the free Project Gutenberg text — which is the Towle — as a fallback, not a first choice.

## A Note on the Illustrations

The novel was first issued in unillustrated serial form in *Le Temps* (1872) and then in a small in-octavo book edition by Hetzel on 30 January 1873. The illustrated edition followed later in 1873 as the eleventh volume of Hetzel's *Voyages Extraordinaires* series, with engravings designed by Alphonse de Neuville (1835–1885) and Léon Benett (1839–1916) and cut by Hildibrand. The American illustrated edition, brought out by James R. Osgood of Boston the same year, used the same Neuville-and-Benett plates — fifty-four of them, plus a small drawing reportedly by Verne. Most cheap modern paperbacks omit them. They are part of the work's original presentation; seek out a copy that reproduces them, or browse the archive.
