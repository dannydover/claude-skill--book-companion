# *Around the World in Eighty Days* by Jules Verne — originally in French

## Why This Book Is Worth Reading (or might not be)

*Around the World in Eighty Days* has lasted because it captures a specific, irretrievable moment — the brief window in human history when the globe first became small enough to circle on a wager but still large enough to be genuinely strange. The novel arrived the instant trans-global travel became real: the First Transcontinental Railroad and the Suez Canal both opened in 1869, the Indian Railways linked up in 1870, and Verne was at his desk within the year. You can feel the giddiness of that window on every page. The book is a love letter to the timetable — to the idea that the world could be conquered not by force but by punctuality, steam, and a properly stamped passport.

What keeps it alive past its historical moment is the propulsive plotting and the strange, almost mathematical purity of Phileas Fogg himself. He is one of the great inscrutable characters in fiction — a man whose only stated emotion is the desire to be on time — and the book's deepest pleasure is watching this human metronome get tangled in places that resist all schedules.

It might not be for you if: you want interiority, ambiguity, or psychological depth. Fogg is a mechanism, not a person, and the novel's other characters are mostly types. Large portions of the non-European journey — through India, Japan, North America — are rendered through a colonial gaze that Verne does not interrogate and that a modern reader has to consciously work around. The prose, even in a good translation, is functional rather than beautiful; Verne was a plotter, not a stylist. If your benchmark for nineteenth-century adventure is *Treasure Island* or *The Count of Monte Cristo*, this compares favorably. If it is *Middlemarch* or *Anna Karenina*, you are reading the wrong book.

### Cultural Impact

The most direct consequence of the novel was real: in 1889, the American journalist Nellie Bly circumnavigated the globe in 72 days specifically to beat Fogg's fictional record, filing dispatches for the *New York World* as she went. Her trip was a global media event — the paper ran a contest asking readers to predict her finish time, receiving over a million entries — and it demonstrated that a work of popular fiction had so thoroughly colonized the public imagination that people felt compelled to settle its premise against the actual world. Few novels have generated that kind of direct response.

The 1956 film adaptation, produced by Michael Todd and starring David Niven as Fogg, won five Academy Awards including Best Picture — at that point the widest-released film in history, shot across 112 locations in thirteen countries. It reintroduced the story to a generation that had not read the book and is partly responsible for the version most people carry in their heads today: Fogg as an English gentleman of impeccable sangfroid, the journey as romantic spectacle.

The title itself passed into the language. "Around the world in X days" is now a productive template for any record-setting circumnavigation — by balloon, bicycle, sailboat, or commercial flight — and the phrase functions independently of any knowledge of Verne. The book also established the globe-trotting race as a recognizable narrative form: the structure of a wager against the clock, a fixed endpoint, and a series of exotic obstacles is the direct ancestor of countless adventure stories, films, and reality television formats.

## Writing the Book

Verne wrote *Around the World in Eighty Days* in Paris in 1872, under conditions that make its relentless optimism stranger and more deliberate than it looks. France had just emerged from the most traumatic eighteen months in its modern history: the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71 had ended in humiliating defeat, the loss of Alsace-Lorraine, and a five-billion-franc indemnity; the Paris Commune had followed immediately, a civil war fought in the streets of the city that left thousands dead and much of central Paris in ruins. Verne himself had relocated his family to Amiens in 1871, partly to escape the chaos. The France in which he sat down to write a novel about a punctual English gentleman outpacing the globe was a country that had recently watched its capital burn.

The novel was written for serialization in the Paris daily *Le Temps*, beginning on 6 November 1872, which meant Verne was writing ahead of publication in real time, with readers following each installment as it appeared. The deadline pressure was structural: the plot's ticking clock was also the author's. His publisher Pierre-Jules Hetzel, who had shaped every *Voyages Extraordinaires* book since *Five Weeks in a Balloon*, pressed Verne hard on pace, and the novel shows it — there is almost no fat on any chapter, and the momentum never lets up. Verne reportedly amused himself and friends by insisting that Fogg would make it in time; according to some accounts, bets were placed.

The connection to the book is audible in retrospect. A country that had just experienced the total failure of its military and administrative machinery — trains commandeered, timetables destroyed, the infrastructure of modern France turned to rubble — was handed a novel in which the machinery works, the timetables hold, and a man who trusts the system completely is vindicated. Whether Verne intended that consolation or simply wrote the book that interested him, the timing gave it an undertone that the first readers would have felt without necessarily naming.

### Recommended English Translations

The historical translation problem is significant: most editions of Verne sold in English from the late nineteenth century through the late twentieth were heavily abridged and frequently inaccurate. Seek out modern scholarly translations.

- *Around the World in Eighty Days*, trans. William Butcher (Oxford World's Classics, 1995). The first critical English translation, with extensive annotations.
- *Around the World in Eighty Days*, trans. Michael Glencross (Penguin Classics, 2004).
- The original George M. Towle translation (1873) remains the most widely reprinted but is dated and occasionally loose.

## A Note on the Illustrations

The illustrated French edition appeared later in 1873 as the 11th volume in Verne's *Voyages Extraordinaires* series, with illustrations by Alphonse de Neuville and Léon Benett. The illustrated US edition that same year contained fifty-four plates by the same pair. Cheap modern paperbacks routinely omit them. ## Then and Now: How This Book Was Read in 1873

Modern readers tend to approach *Around the World in Eighty Days* as a children's adventure or a charmingly antique romp. This is almost the opposite of how its first readers experienced it.

To a French reader in late 1872, the novel was something close to contemporary realism. The story began serialization in *Le Temps* on 6 November 1872. The story was published in installments over the next 45 days, with its ending timed to synchronize Fogg's 21 December deadline with the real world. Verne and his publisher had engineered something genuinely new: a piece of fiction whose plot was synchronized in real time with the calendar of the reader. Each morning's installment landed on the breakfast table on roughly the day Fogg was supposed to be at that point in his journey. As it was being published serially for the first time, some readers believed that the journey was actually taking place — bets were placed, and some railway companies and ship liner companies lobbied Verne to appear in the book. A modern analogy might be a podcast or streaming series that drops episodes in sync with real events its audience is tracking — the immersive frisson of "is this happening?" was central to the original experience, and is almost completely lost in book form today.

The political backdrop has also faded. France in 1872 was a humiliated nation. The Franco-Prussian War had ended badly less than two years earlier, the Paris Commune had been crushed in 1871, and France had lost Alsace-Lorraine. National confidence was fragile. Into this mood Verne dropped a novel in which a Frenchman (Passepartout) is the warm, resourceful, brave human heart of a journey otherwise driven by a chilly Englishman's money. The book's gentle teasing of English rigidity — Fogg's clockwork affect, the Reform Club's complacency — would have read very differently to a Parisian audience smarting from defeat than it does to us. Verne is not anti-English (he loved English literature), but he is making a quiet case for French human warmth against English mechanical efficiency, and his original readers caught that immediately.

The technological context is equally dimmed for us. To readers of 1872, the very premise of the book was a current-events claim, not a fantasy. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 had been one of the great spectacles of the age. The completion of the American transcontinental railroad the same year was front-page news worldwide. The linking of the Indian rail network in 1870 was the third leg. In interviews in 1894 and 1904, Verne says the source was "through reading one day in a Paris cafe" and "due merely to a tourist advertisement seen by chance in the columns of a newspaper." The novel was, in effect, asking its readers to test a claim being made by actual travel companies in actual newspapers: can you really do this now? The answer mattered to the original audience in a way it cannot matter to us.

The colonial frame, which today reads as the book's most uncomfortable feature, was for the original audience simply the texture of the world. The European reader of 1872 took for granted that the planet was knit together by a network of British, French, and American steamers and railways operated by Europeans for European convenience, and that the local populations were, narratively, scenery and obstacle. Verne is no more or less colonial than his readership; what dates is not his prejudice but the unselfconsciousness of it. A modern reader has to do interpretive work that the original reader did not: to see the colonial machinery as machinery, rather than as the natural order.

Finally, what is now invisible: the original audience read Verne as a modernist, in the literal sense — a writer of the modern, the contemporary, the just-now-possible. He was neither nostalgic nor futuristic. He was reporting on the present and finding it astonishing. The ubiquitous label "father of science fiction" is a posthumous one, attached to him in the twentieth century by Hugo Gernsback and others, and it has done genuine damage to how Verne is read. *Eighty Days* contains no science fiction at all. Every train, ship, and elephant in the novel was available for hire in 1872. The wonder Verne wants you to feel is not the wonder of imagined futures but the wonder of the actual present — the realization that this is where we now live, a planet whose far side is eighty days away. To recover the book's original force, try reading it not as an old adventure but as a piece of journalism about a world that had just become, for the first time in human history, finite.
