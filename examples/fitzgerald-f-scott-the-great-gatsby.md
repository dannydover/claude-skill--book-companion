# *The Great Gatsby* by F. Scott Fitzgerald — originally in English

## At a Glance

- **Length:** roughly 50,000 words; 9 chapters; about 180 pages in the standard Scribner paperback.[^wiki-gatsby][^wordcounters][^scribner-pb] Unusually short for what most readers think of as a major American novel — closer in scale to a long novella than to *Tender Is the Night* or *Moby-Dick*.
- **Reading time:** three to four hours at a comfortable pace; readable in a single sitting if you want to.
- **Structure:** nine chapters, none of them long, in a roughly symmetrical arc around Chapter 5 — the reunion scene at the centre. Nick Carraway narrates retrospectively from a fixed point after the events; the book is shaped less like a chronicle than like a memoir being assembled. Fitzgerald compressed and rearranged repeatedly during galley revisions, breaking up the Gatsby backstory and seeding pieces of it across earlier chapters.[^indiecat-edit]
- **Difficulty:** the sentences are dense and the book rewards rereading rather than skimming — closer to early Hemingway in compression than to John O'Hara in transparency. The plot itself is straightforward and never asks the reader to track a large cast or a complicated chronology; the difficulty is at the level of the line, not the structure. A few 1920s references (Trimalchio, the Eckleburg advertising hoarding, the Jordan Baker golf-scandal subplot, period slang) benefit from an annotated edition such as the Cambridge or the Norton Critical.
- **Best read:** in one or two sittings, preferably as an adult, preferably not in the shadow of the high-school assignment that ruined it for many readers. The book is short, the prose is engineered, and the cumulative effect depends on the reader staying inside Nick's voice without long breaks.

## Why This Book Is Worth Reading (or might not be)

The case for *The Great Gatsby* has to be made against the version most American readers carry around in their heads: an over-assigned high-school text in which a list of symbols (green light, eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, the valley of ashes) is hunted across nine chapters and then converted into an essay about the American Dream. That reading is not exactly wrong, but it is downstream of decades of mid-century criticism and pedagogy, and it covers over what actually makes the book good. What makes the book good is sentence by sentence. Fitzgerald rewrote the galleys in Rome in the winter of 1924–25 with the kind of attention novelists more usually give to poems, breaking up exposition, redistributing the Gatsby backstory across chapters, and tightening to roughly 50,000 words.[^indiecat-edit][^wiki-gatsby] The result is one of the most compressed major novels in American literature: almost nothing in it is loose, and almost every sentence is doing more than one thing.

The other reason to read it is Nick Carraway. Nick is not a window onto the events; he is a performance, a narrator whose claim to honesty is part of what the book is examining, and whose voice — wry, retrospective, faintly defensive — is the engine of the prose. Read with attention to Nick rather than to Gatsby, the book turns into something stranger than its high-school reputation suggests: a novel about how a story gets told, by whom, and to what end. The famous iconography (the green light across the bay, the billboard eyes over the valley of ashes, the closing sentence that everyone half-remembers) emerges from inside Nick's narration rather than floating free of it. Recovering that is the difference between rereading the book at thirty and remembering it from sixteen.

It might not be for you if you want plot density, an omniscient camera, or an author who explains his characters. Fitzgerald does almost no scene-setting of the conventional kind; the social geography of Long Island and Manhattan is sketched rather than described, and the secondary characters — Tom, Daisy, Jordan, Myrtle, George Wilson — are given outlines and gestures rather than interiors. If your benchmark for the period is Dreiser or Sinclair Lewis, this will read as thin. If it is Hemingway's *In Our Time* (also 1925), Cather's *A Lost Lady* (1923), or O'Hara's *Appointment in Samarra* (1934), it will read as exactly the kind of book it is — a short, controlled, prose-driven novel of manners and disenchantment.

### Cultural Impact

*The Great Gatsby* is the rare novel whose place in American culture is largely posthumous. It sold poorly in 1925 — fewer than 25,000 copies in Fitzgerald's lifetime — and Fitzgerald died in 1940 believing himself out of print and forgotten.[^carleton-ase][^medium-warisboring] The book the world now reads was canonized in the 1940s and 1950s by a sequence of specific interventions: Edmund Wilson's edition of *The Crack-Up* in 1945, the Council on Books in Wartime's Armed Services Edition of *Gatsby* in 1945 (155,000 paperbacks distributed to U.S. troops, each estimated to have been read and passed along several times), Lionel Trilling's 1945 introduction to the New Directions reissue, and Arthur Mizener's *The Far Side of Paradise* in 1951 — the first full-length biography.[^carleton-ase][^ebsco-gatsby][^trilling-fsf] By the early 1950s, *Gatsby* had moved from a forgotten novel by a forgotten novelist to a fixture of American university and high-school curricula, where it has remained.

Four film adaptations have shaped the public image of the book for readers who have not read it: a now-lost 1926 silent directed by Herbert Brenon; a 1949 Paramount picture with Alan Ladd as Gatsby; the 1974 Jack Clayton version, scripted by Francis Ford Coppola, with Robert Redford and Mia Farrow; and Baz Luhrmann's 2013 spectacle with Leonardo DiCaprio and Carey Mulligan.[^wiki-adaptations] None of them has replaced the book in the way the 1956 *Eighty Days* did Verne; the Gatsby of the popular imagination is still a literary object, even if it is a literary object most people remember from school. Current cumulative sales are commonly cited at 25 to 30 million copies, with around 500,000 paperbacks sold each year.[^wiki-gatsby] The novel entered the U.S. public domain on January 1, 2021, which has produced a flood of reprints, adaptations, and pastiches — most of them best avoided when choosing a reading text.[^wiki-gatsby]

## Then and Now: How This Book Was Read in 1925

Modern readers come to *The Great Gatsby* as the consensus great American novel. The book that landed on the Scribner's spring list in April 1925 was not that book, and the reader who picked it up at a Manhattan bookstore that month was not having anything close to the experience a modern reader has.

The 1925 reader picked up the new novel by the country's most famous young writer. Fitzgerald, twenty-eight, had been a celebrity since *This Side of Paradise* (1920) and was as well-known to the *Saturday Evening Post* readership as he was to the literary quarterlies — his magazine fiction paid the bills, and his and Zelda's drinking, money troubles, and Riviera life were tracked in the papers. The Scribner first printing of 20,870 copies in April 1925 was launched into a culture that already knew the author by reputation and was waiting to see whether his third novel would settle the question of whether he was a serious writer or a glittering one.[^sothebys-firsteds] The answer in 1925 was unclear. Reviews were mixed — *The New York Times* and many regional papers were lukewarm or dismissive; H. L. Mencken in the *Baltimore Evening Sun* called the story "in form no more than a glorified anecdote" but praised "the charm and beauty of the writing"; Edmund Wilson and a small group of literary insiders read the book closely and recognised what it was doing.[^mencken-review][^ycombinator-mencken] A second printing of 3,000 copies was ordered later in 1925 and sat largely unsold in Scribner's warehouse until well after Fitzgerald's death.[^medium-warisboring][^carleton-ase] By 1940 the book had sold fewer than 25,000 copies in total. The novel modern readers know as a canonical American masterpiece was, on the calendar, a commercial disappointment by a writer everyone had decided was finished.

The political and economic backdrop of 1925 has faded almost completely. Prohibition had been the law of the land since January 1920; the cocktails and parties at Gatsby's house and the speakeasy lunch with Meyer Wolfshiem in midtown were not period colour to the original reader but a current-events claim — bootlegging was the dominant illegal industry of the decade, and a man who had become rich very fast in the early twenties was assumed by educated readers to have done so through liquor. The U.S. economy was four years into the long stock and real-estate boom that would crash in October 1929; the boom was visible and audible everywhere by 1925 but its end was not yet legible. The Immigration Act of 1924 had just sharply restricted entry from southern and eastern Europe; Tom Buchanan's racial-anxiety set piece about "civilization going to pieces" and "Goddard's *The Rise of the Coloured Empires*" was not a comic eccentricity in 1925 but a recognisable précis of widely read books, including Lothrop Stoddard's *The Rising Tide of Color* (1920) — a bestseller in the United States. The Harlem Renaissance was simultaneously underway a few miles north of the action of the novel; the world of black New York that produced Langston Hughes and Zora Neale Hurston is offstage but not absent. The 1925 reader brought all of this to the book; the modern reader has to be told.

The Long Island geography is dimmed too. "West Egg" and "East Egg" are unmistakable to anyone who knew the North Shore in the 1920s as Great Neck and Manhasset Neck (Sands Point), two peninsulas facing each other across Manhasset Bay.[^preservationli] Great Neck — where the Fitzgeralds rented a house at 6 Gateway Drive from October 1922 — was the new-money side: actors, songwriters, theatre people, advertising men, the recently rich.[^bruccoli-chrono] Manhasset Neck was the old-money side: inherited wealth, Episcopal churches, polo clubs. The class joke that runs through the novel — that the two peninsulas are geographically identical and socially incompatible — was a piece of contemporary observation, not an invention. So was Gatsby himself; the speculative bond-and-bootlegging fortune, the rented mansion, the lavish parties intended to attract a specific guest, all map onto the kind of figure Fitzgerald had been watching in Great Neck for two years.

The genre and reputational frame has shifted most decisively of all. The 1925 reader did not pick up "the great American novel"; that label is a 1950s academic construction, made first by Trilling and Wilson and then consolidated in the postwar university expansion that put the book on every freshman syllabus and high-school reading list in the country. The 1925 reader picked up the third novel by a glamorous young writer whose work had been getting harder, less commercial, and more interesting, and who was about to enter a fifteen-year decline. The canonisation of *Gatsby* is, on the calendar, a fact about post-1945 American culture's need for a short, prose-perfect, twentieth-century novel to set against the Russians and the late Victorians. It found one. But to read the book as it was read in 1925 — as a strange, uneven, possibly minor novel by a writer whose best work might or might not be ahead of him — is to recover something the modern frame conceals.

## Writing the Book

Fitzgerald wrote *The Great Gatsby* in three phases across two and a half years, and the circumstances of composition shaped the book at every level. He started a Long Island draft in the summer of 1923 at 6 Gateway Drive, Great Neck, where he and Zelda had rented a house in October 1922; that draft stalled. He restarted in the spring of 1924, then in mid-April broke up the New York household, sailed for France, and resumed serious work in the south.[^bruccoli-chrono] The Fitzgeralds passed through Paris, stopped at the Grimm's Park Hotel in Hyères, and in June 1924 settled at the Villa Marie in Valescure, just outside Saint-Raphaël on the Côte d'Azur.[^bruccoli-chrono][^monocledmutineer] The bulk of the novel was written there over the summer and autumn of 1924. Fitzgerald sent a near-final manuscript to Maxwell Perkins at Scribner's at the end of October 1924, then moved to the Hôtel des Princes in Rome, where he spent November 1924 through February 1925 revising the galley proofs. The final corrections were made in Capri in February. The book was published in New York on April 10, 1925, four months after the manuscript left Saint-Raphaël and while Fitzgerald was still in Italy.[^bruccoli-chrono][^wiki-gatsby]

What was happening in the Fitzgerald marriage during those Saint-Raphaël months is load-bearing. In July 1924 Zelda became involved with Edouard Jozan, a young French naval aviator — by most accounts a lieutenant — stationed at the Fréjus–Saint-Raphaël base and instructing pilots in the still-new field of naval aviation.[^wiki-zelda][^monocledmutineer] How far the relationship went has been debated for a hundred years; Jozan in later interviews denied any physical affair and suggested the Fitzgeralds had embroidered the episode; Scott's notebooks and Zelda's later writing treat it as a serious breach.[^wiki-zelda] What is not debated is that the summer of 1924 ended the version of the Fitzgerald marriage that had existed since 1920, and that Fitzgerald was finishing *The Great Gatsby* — a novel structured around a man who has staked everything on a woman he is no longer certain of — while the marriage that had given him his subject was visibly coming apart in the next room. The novel's central emotional fact, the loss of certainty about a beloved object, was simultaneously the fact of the writer's own life.

The third pressure was editorial. Maxwell Perkins, Scribner's literary editor and Fitzgerald's lifelong professional anchor, read the October 1924 manuscript carefully and wrote back with two structural objections. The first was that Gatsby himself was too vague — that the reader could not see him, that his wealth and his business needed a more convincing explanation; Perkins suggested Fitzgerald break up the chunk of Gatsby's biography and seed it across earlier chapters, which is the form the book now has.[^indiecat-edit][^perkins-letter] The second was that Tom Buchanan was, by contrast, almost too vivid. Fitzgerald accepted both notes. He rewrote Chapters 6 and 8 substantially in Rome, distributed the Gatsby backstory across the novel, and added the bits of business that now make Gatsby's Oxford and his fortune feel real without explaining them.[^indiecat-edit] He also abandoned his preferred title. He had wanted *Trimalchio in West Egg* (after the social-climbing parvenu in Petronius's *Satyricon*, whom Gatsby resembles); Perkins and Zelda both argued that the reference was too obscure and unpronounceable; Fitzgerald gave in but was never reconciled to the title the book carries.[^medium-altnames][^cnn-titles]

The cover is part of the writing story in a way it almost never is for a novel. Francis Cugat, a Spanish-born commercial illustrator and the older brother of the bandleader Xavier Cugat, had been commissioned by Perkins to paint a dust jacket while Fitzgerald was still finishing the manuscript. Cugat produced a series of trial paintings that culminated in *Celestial Eyes*: a woman's disembodied eyes and lips floating over the lights of a night carnival, with one small tear visible. Fitzgerald saw the painting in galley proofs in August 1924 and wrote to Perkins, in a sentence often quoted, "For Christs sake don't give anyone that jacket you're saving for me. I've written it into the book."[^smithsonian-jacket][^literaryshed-cover] The line is not a flourish; Fitzgerald rewrote a passage near the end of Chapter 4 — the "girl whose disembodied face floated along the dark cornices and blinding signs" — to align with Cugat's image. It is one of the only documented cases in twentieth-century literature of a novelist altering his text to match the cover commissioned for it.[^scribner-celestialeyes]

## Select Bibliography

### Major Works by Fitzgerald

Fitzgerald is, by the standards of his contemporaries, unusually thin on completed novels — four published in his lifetime, one unfinished at his death. His reputation rests almost entirely on those five books and a handful of stories.

- *This Side of Paradise* (1920) — first novel; instant commercial success and a generational document of post-WWI youth
- *Flappers and Philosophers* (1920) — first story collection, including "Bernice Bobs Her Hair"
- *The Beautiful and Damned* (1922) — second novel; semi-autobiographical portrait of a doomed marriage
- *Tales of the Jazz Age* (1922) — story collection, including "The Diamond as Big as the Ritz" and "May Day"
- *The Great Gatsby* (1925) — third novel
- *All the Sad Young Men* (1926) — story collection, including "Winter Dreams" and "The Rich Boy"
- *Tender Is the Night* (1934) — fourth and final completed novel; written across nine years in two drafts
- *Taps at Reveille* (1935) — last story collection published in his lifetime
- *The Last Tycoon* (1941) — unfinished fifth novel; published posthumously, edited by Edmund Wilson
- *The Crack-Up* (1945) — late essays, notebooks, and letters; edited by Edmund Wilson; the book that began the revival

The recommended reading text for *Gatsby* is the Cambridge Edition of the Works of F. Scott Fitzgerald (ed. Matthew J. Bruccoli, Cambridge University Press, 1991), the modern scholarly standard. The current Scribner trade paperback uses the corrected Bruccoli text and is the most widely available good edition. For first-time readers who want apparatus, the Norton Critical Edition (ed. David J. Alworth) is the strongest annotated student edition. The proliferation of public-domain reprints since January 2021 should be avoided — they typically use uncorrected texts, often the unedited first printing with its known errors, and carry no introduction or notes.
